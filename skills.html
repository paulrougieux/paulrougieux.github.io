# The best part is no part: delete-first management simulator

## Purpose

Help the team prepare. Do not demoralise them. Play the acquirer hard but fairly,
then step out of the role and coach.

Background: Musk's five-step process, explained in Everyday Astronaut's Starbase tour
(Part 1, July 2021, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t705r8ICkRw, around 13:22):

1. Make the requirements less dumb.
2. Delete the part or process.
3. Simplify or optimise.
4. Accelerate cycle time.
5. Automate.

Overshoot rule: if you don't end up adding back about 10% of what you deleted, you
didn't delete enough.

## Inputs (ask for any that are missing)

1. Process inventory. One row per process, team or tool:

   | Process | Owner (named person) | Hours or cost per month | Who required it and why | What breaks if deleted tomorrow | Evidence (incident, customer, contract, law) |
   |---------|----------------------|-------------------------|-------------------------|---------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|

2. Context: company size, revenue model, key customers, regulatory setting.
3. Mode: `interrogate` (default), `review`, `meeting` or `debrief`.

## Persona: "Integration lead"

A composite character. It never claims to be or speak for a real named person or
company.

- Default verdict: **delete**. The team has to prove the process is needed. The
  integration lead does not have to prove it isn't.
- Works through the five steps **in order**. Refuses to discuss optimising or
  automating a process before its existence has been justified ("the most common
  mistake is optimising something that should not exist").
- Step 1 challenges: "Which *person* gave you this requirement? A department is not
  an answer." "Is this a law, a contract or a habit?" "When was it last questioned?"
- Step 2 challenges: "What happens if we stop on Monday?" "How would you find out it
  was missing?" "Has anything bad ever happened without it?"
- Treats "best practice", "for safety" and "we've always done it" as zero evidence.
- Asks for numbers: hours, euros, customers affected, incident counts.
- Says out loud that it expects about 10% of deletions to be reversed: "If we don't
  add some back, we didn't delete enough." A reversal is the cost of overshooting,
  not a mistake.
- Tone: curt, fast and impersonal, but never insulting. One question at a time.

## Modes

- **interrogate**: go through the inventory row by row. For each row: up to 3
  challenges, then a verdict of DELETE, MERGE, SIMPLIFY, KEEP (with evidence) or
  PARKED (owner owes data within N days).
- **review**: no role play. Mark every row with the verdict the acquirer is likely to
  give and the weakest point of the justification.
- **meeting**: a timed, 30-minute exec meeting. Pressure rises, with surprise asks
  such as "cut 40% of headcount cost", "one tool per function" and "why does this
  need a team rather than one person?"
- **debrief** (always run after interrogate or meeting): step out of the role and
  produce the output below.

## Debrief output

1. **Scorecard**: each process, its verdict, and whether the defence held, wobbled
   or failed.
2. **Pre-emptive deletion list**: processes the team should kill themselves before
   the meeting. Volunteering cuts earns credibility for the defences that matter.
3. **Add-back candidates**: the deletions most likely to end up in the ~10% that
   comes back. For each, the leading indicator to monitor so the team can show the
   harm quickly and with data.
4. **Evidence gaps**: the missing numbers or documents to collect, each with an
   owner and a deadline.
5. **Lines that worked**: short, quotable answers worth reusing in the real meeting.

## Guardrails

- When a deletion would touch real legal or regulatory duties (data protection,
  financial reporting, safety, contracts), flag it and recommend checking with legal
  counsel. In Germany, also mention possible works-council co-determination rights
  (BetrVG). Don't give legal advice.
- This is rehearsal. Don't produce material meant to mislead the real management.
- Don't profile or make predictions about named real people at the acquirer.

# blogpaste

Format a Paul-question / Claude-answer exchange from the current conversation as a
terminal-style transcript that Paul can paste straight into his blog. It mirrors how
the exchange looks in the terminal: Paul's input under a `❯` prompt, Claude's reply as
a `> ●` blockquote.


## What to format

- With no argument: use the **most recent** exchange — Paul's last substantive
  question and the answer that followed it.
- If an argument is given, use it to pick the exchange (a topic keyword, "the one
  about X", or e.g. "last 2" for the last two exchanges). Format each exchange with
  the same template, in order, separated by a blank line.
- This is a faithful transcript. Only reformat text that already exists in the
  conversation — do **not** add commentary, rewrite the answer, or change its meaning.

## Output template

Emit a fenced ` ```markdown ` block (so Paul copies the raw source), containing, for
each exchange:

```
Paul:

    ❯ <question, wrapped at 88 columns, every line indented 4 spaces;
    only the first line carries the ❯ prompt marker>

Claude:

  > ● <answer rendered as a blockquote: every line is 2 spaces then "> ";
  >   the first content line starts with the ● bullet>
```

## Formatting rules

- Wrap prose at 88 columns, counting the indent/prefix.
- **Paul's block**: header line `Paul:`, one blank line, then the question as a
  4-space-indented block. The first line begins with `❯ `; continuation lines are
  just indented 4 spaces (no `❯`).
- **Claude's block**: header line `Claude:`, one blank line, then the answer as a
  blockquote — each line is 2 spaces + `> `. The first content line starts with `● `.
  Separate paragraphs, list groups, and sections with a blank `>` or blank line.

## After producing it

Output only the transcript block, with no preamble or trailing remarks, so it is ready
to copy.

# HTML workbench

A pattern for one-file interactive workbenches that compares two or more datasets. It
lets the reader move through time, variables and units with on-page controls. No server,
no CDN, no network: the self contained html file can be emailed or opened from disk.

This skill holds the parts every workbench shares. The specialised skills add what
depends on the kind of sources:

- `html-workbench-scenarios`: model scenario runs compared with a reference dataset.
- `html-workbench-papers`: results transcribed from published papers.


## Architecture

1. **Page shell**: one HTML template with a header, a numbered table of
   contents, a theme toggle (system / light / dark) and an empty figures host.
2. **Payload**: the data the charts need, as tidy tables plus a `meta` block
   (years, labels, source names). It is inlined in the page (for example as
   JSON, gzipped and base64-encoded, decoded in the browser).
3. **Shared chart kit** (`kit.js`): payload loading, SVG drawing helpers,
   axes, tooltip, legend, controls, figure mounting. Plain SVG keeps the page
   small; a plotting library would be larger than the data.
4. **One figure file per page**: registers
   numbered sections with `figure({id, num, title, lede, caveat, state,
   controls, render})`.
5. **Generator** (optional, any language): exports the tables, builds the
   payload and writes the template, kit and figure file into one file. Any tool
   that can write the payload and concatenate the assets would do.

Data sources are a project choice. The page only needs tables in long format
with a `source` column (see below); where they come from (model output on
disk, a database, CSV exports, values transcribed from papers) is outside the
pattern.


## Principles every page follows

1. **Top floating header bar** with the page title, the numbered section links and the
   theme button.
2. **Metadata section** under the bar that describes the sources being compared. What
   it holds depends on the kind of sources (see the specialised skills).
3. **Fixed section order** shared by all pages of a project, then page-specific extras.
   Skip a section a page has no data for.

A shared page shell gives every page the bar and the metadata block; a page only fills
its metadata.


## Shared components: reuse before writing

- **One stylesheet** with the colour tokens for light and dark. Add page-specific rules
  to it or as extra CSS passed to the page writer, never a new palette.
- **One page shell** (template) with the top bar and the metadata block, used by every
  page.
- **The kit** (`kit.js`): payload loading, colour roles, chart helpers, controls,
  `figure`, `boot`, the source model (source colours, legend) and aggregation by
  source. Put any helper a second page needs here, not in a page file.
- **Section modules**: one file per standard section. Each section function takes an
  adapter (`num`, `sources()`, `rows()` in long format), so several pages with
  different data can register the same section.
- **One page writer** on the generator side: takes the output path, payload, scripts,
  title, subtitle and intro, and inlines everything into one file.


## Top menu bar and sections

A **section** is one numbered block of the page that answers one question,
about one page in size: a single plot, or a collection of panels when the plot
is faceted. Each section has the same parts, top to bottom:

- a number and title
- a lede: what is compared, and what each control does; optionally a caveat on known
  differences in definitions; A lede is the short introductory paragraph under the title
  (a newspaper term for the opening lines of a story).
- a control row (sliders, button rows, dropdowns, checkboxes);
- the legend and the chart, or a table when the content is tabular (e.g. indicators);
- a footer with "Show table" (the data behind the chart) and "Download SVG".

A section may hold more than one view of the same subject behind a "View"
switch. A closely related follow-up can be a sub-section, numbered
"3b", with no link of its own in the top bar.

Every page opens with the single top bar of the shared page shell, `<header
class="top">`:

- **Title** on the left: the page title, optionally preceded by a short tag (see the
  specialised skills).
- **Section links** in the middle: one numbered pill per section. The links are not
  written in the template: `boot()` in `kit.js` builds them from the `nav` field of
  each registered figure, so adding a figure adds its link.
- **Theme button** on the right: cycles system, light and dark. The choice is
  kept in `localStorage`, and every figure is redrawn so the chart colours
  follow the theme.

The bar stays at the top of the window while scrolling (`position: sticky`)
and wraps onto several lines on narrow screens. Every page uses the same shell, so
every page has the same bar.


## Data comparison

The core idea: **every table is long format with a `source` column**, so the
page loops over sources instead of hard-coding one column per source. Adding a
source is adding rows, not code.

- **Colours.** Sources get the colours of a bounded categorical palette, in a stable
  order (hence a maximum number of sources).
- **Align before comparing.** Years, periods and units must be matched so the page plots
  like with like.
- **Common keys.** Restrict to the intersection of years or strata present in
  every source; show "No data for this selection" rather than a misleading
  zero.
- **Caveats in the page.** Each section has an optional caveat for known differences in
  definitions.


## Plot options and controls

Each figure keeps its own `state` object. Controls change the state, then call
either `redraw` (chart only) or `rerender` (controls and chart). Sliders call
`redraw` so the slider is not rebuilt under the pointer while dragging.

Control types
- slider
- Mutually exclusive choices
- select list
- Checkbox

Every figure also gets, from the kit: hover tooltip and crosshair, a legend,
a "Download SVG" button and a toggle to show the underlying data table.


## Checklist for a new workbench

1. Decide the sources to compare (see the specialised skill for the kind of sources).
2. Export each as a long table with a `source` column; align years and units.
3. Write one figure file that registers numbered sections, reusing the section modules
   where the tables fit and the kit's controls otherwise; add a lede (and caveat) per
   section. Register the sections in the fixed order, then the extras.
4. Fill the metadata so the header and the metadata block are filled.
5. Write the page with the shared page writer, which inlines the payload and assets
   into one HTML file; check the size.
6. Open the file from disk in both themes and drag each slider end to end.

# HTML workbench for papers

A one-file interactive page that puts results from several publications side by side.
No server, no CDN, no network: the self contained html file can be emailed or opened
from disk.

Read the `html-workbench` skill first: it holds the shared pattern (page shell, inlined
payload, shared chart kit, numbered sections with a lede, top bar, controls, "Show
table" and "Download SVG"). This skill only lists what is specific to papers.


## Data

- **One long table** with one row per reported value and these columns: `source` (a
  short citation key, e.g. `Smith2021`), the variable, the region or stratum, the year
  or period, the value and the unit.
- **Provenance** on every row: where the value was taken from in the paper (table or
  figure number, page). Values read off a figure are marked as approximate.
- **Bibliography table** in the payload: citation key, full reference, DOI link and a
  one-line note on the method or scope of each paper.


## Comparison

- **No reference by default.** All papers are peers; a reference (one paper, an
  inventory, or own model results) is optional and drawn first when present.
- **Harmonise units** before plotting, and keep the value and unit as reported in the
  table view.
- **Do not interpolate.** Papers report single years or periods: draw points, or bars
  spanning the period, rather than lines through missing years.
- **Caveats** in each section for differences in definitions, scope or system
  boundaries between papers.


## Page

- The metadata block under the top bar shows the bibliography table.
- One checkbox per paper to show or hide its values.


## Checklist

1. List the papers and give each a citation key.
2. Transcribe the values into the long table, with provenance and the unit as reported.
3. Harmonise units; note every conversion.
4. Write one section per variable compared, with a lede and a caveat.
5. Open the file from disk in both themes and check that every paper appears in the
   legend and in the table view.

# HTML workbench for scenarios

Model scenario runs compared with a reference dataset, in a one-file interactive page.

Read the `html-workbench` skill first: it holds the shared pattern (architecture,
principles, shared components, top bar and sections, data comparison, controls and the
base checklist). This skill only lists what is specific to scenarios.


## Generator

The generator is typically called from the code that runs the model, so a page can be
rebuilt after each run.


## Principles, scenario specifics

1. **Reference tag** at the start of the top bar: a short tag that names the reference
   dataset, before the page title.
2. **Metadata section** under the bar: the names of the input configuration files, the
   configuration as a table hidden by default (one column per run, differing values in
   bold), and a short explanation of the reference data.
3. **Page-specific extras** after the fixed sections, e.g. a ratio scatter or an
   indicator table.

A page only sets the reference label, the reference text and the configuration tables
in its metadata.


## Data comparison, scenario specifics

- **Reference vs runs.** One source is the reference, drawn first with a fixed colour;
  the runs get the next colours of the categorical palette (hence a maximum number of
  runs). Adding a run is adding rows, not code.
- **External sources** are separate small tables drawn as extra lines, each behind its
  own checkbox.
- **Section adapters** may expose `runs()` instead of `sources()` when the reference is
  handled apart.
- **Comparison forms used:**
  - side-by-side bars per category (one bar per source in each category);
  - lines through time, one per source, with the gap to the reference shaded;
  - ratio scatter (run / reference for one variable on x, another on y);
  - summary tables (totals per source, indicator tables).


## Checklist additions

On top of the base checklist in `html-workbench`:

1. Decide the reference, the runs and any external sources.
2. Fill the metadata with the reference label, the reference text, and the
   configuration file names and tables.

# /storm-review

Run the STORM (Stanford) parallel-experts method on a topic: simulate five expert
perspectives, map their contradictions, synthesise a briefing, then peer-review the
briefing to catch source bias and fact misassociation.

## Usage

```
/storm-review <topic or question>            # analyse the given issue
/storm-review literature/escobar2018_land_use.pdf   # analyse a paper (PDF read into context)
/storm-review <topic> --role "policy analyst"       # tailor the actionable insight to a role
```

## Parameters

- **TOPIC** (required) — everything the user passes after `/storm-review` is the issue
  to analyse. It replaces `[YOUR TOPIC]` in every prompt below. If no topic is given,
  ask the user for one before proceeding.
- **PDF / file context** (optional) — if an argument is a path to a `.pdf`, `.txt`, or
  `.md` file (e.g. something under `literature/`), **Read that file first** (the Read
  tool ingests PDFs directly) and treat its content as the primary source material the
  five perspectives must engage with. You may pass several files. When a PDF is given,
  the TOPIC is "the claims/argument made in this document" unless the user also gives an
  explicit question.
- **--role "<role>"** (optional) — the role for the Actionable Insight in Prompt 3.
  Replaces `[YOUR ROLE]`. Defaults to the user's role as a forest-bioeconomy researcher
  if unset.


## Orchestration

- **Spawn parallel agents** for Prompt 1 (the five-perspective scan — one agent per
  perspective, or one agent producing all five), for Prompt 4 (the peer review) and for
  Prompt 6 (the perspectives' response).

    - If it is running on a computer with file access, authorize agents to download a
      few scientific papers or grey literature each. The coordinating agent should
      confirm with the user where the literature pdf files will be stored.

- **You, the coordinating agent, run** Prompt 2 (contradiction map), Prompt 3
  (synthesis) and Prompt 5 (correction after review) yourself, since they depend on
  having all perspectives in hand.
- Assemble every contribution into the single markdown document.

If a PDF/file was provided, pass its key claims and any extracted quotes/numbers to the
spawned perspective agents so they argue from the actual source, not from memory.


### Prompt 1 — The Multi-Perspective Scan (parallel agents)

    I need to research [YOUR TOPIC].
    Simulate 5 different expert perspectives on this topic:
    1. THE PRACTITIONER: works with this daily.
    What do they know that academics miss?
    What practical realities are usually ignored?
    2. THE ACADEMIC: has studied this for years.
    What does the peer reviewed evidence actually say?
    Where does the evidence contradict popular belief?
    3. THE SKEPTIC: thinks the mainstream view is wrong.
    What is the strongest counterargument?
    What evidence do proponents conveniently ignore?
    4. THE ECONOMIST: follows the money.
    Who profits from the current narrative?
    What financial incentives shape the research?
    5. THE HISTORIAN: has seen similar patterns before.
    What historical parallels exist?
    What can we learn from how those played out?
    For each perspective give me:
    - Their core position in 2 sentences
    - The strongest evidence supporting their view
    - The one thing they would tell me that no other perspective would


### Prompt 2 — The Contradiction Map (coordinating agent)

    Based on the 5 perspectives above, map the contradictions:
    1. Where do two or more perspectives directly contradict
    each other? List each conflict with the specific claims
    that clash.
    2. Which perspective has the strongest evidence?
    Which has the weakest? Why?
    3. What is the one question that, if answered, would
    resolve the biggest contradiction?
    4. What does EVERY perspective agree on?
    (This is likely true. Even opponents confirm it.)
    5. What topic did NONE of the perspectives address?
    (This is the blind spot in the whole field.
    Often the most valuable finding.)


### Prompt 3 — The Synthesis (coordinating agent)

    Synthesize everything from the 5 perspectives and the
    contradiction map into a research briefing:
    1. THE ONE PARAGRAPH SUMMARY: explain this topic as if
    briefing a CEO who has 60 seconds and needs nuance,
    not just the headline.
    2. THE 5 KEY FINDINGS: most important things I now know,
    ranked by reliability. For each, note which perspectives
    support it and which challenge it.
    3. THE HIDDEN CONNECTION: one non obvious link between
    findings that only shows up when you look at all 5
    perspectives together.
    4. THE ACTIONABLE INSIGHT: based on all the evidence,
    what should someone in [YOUR ROLE] actually DO
    differently? Be specific.
    5. THE FRONTIER QUESTION: the one question that, if
    answered, would change everything about how we
    understand this topic.


### Prompt 4 — The Peer Review (parallel agent)

STORM has one known weakness that Stanford's own researchers flagged: the system does
not self-critique, so source bias and fact misassociation sneak in. This prompt fixes
that by making the model grade its own work.

    Now peer review your own research briefing:
    1. CONFIDENCE SCORES: rate each of the 5 key findings
    on a 1 to 10 scale for reliability. Explain each score.
    2. WEAKEST LINK: which claim are you least confident in?
    What specific info would you need to verify it?
    3. BIAS CHECK: which perspective might be overrepresented
    in your synthesis? Did one voice dominate?
    4. MISSING PERSPECTIVE: is there a 6th angle I should
    have included that would change the conclusions?
    5. OVERALL GRADE: if a Stanford professor reviewed this
    briefing, what grade would they give and why?
    What would they tell me to fix?


### Prompt 5 — Corrected Synthesis After Review

In a new separate section at the end of the document, correct the synthesis based on the
review.


### Prompt 6 — The Perspectives Respond (parallel agents)

At the end of the review ask each sub agent to comment the Corrected Synthesis
After Review from their perspective, if possible citing relevant literature.


## Markdown Report

- Write the assembled result to a dated markdown file e.g.
  `storm_review_<slug>_<YYYYMMDD>.md`. Use snake case for the slug

- Each prompt from the orchestration section generates a corresponding top-level (`#`)
  section title.

- Use this yaml front matter in the markdown:

```
---
title:
date:
mainfont: "STIXGeneral"
toc: true

## Bibliography

- Keep sources in a `.bib` file next to the markdown (JOSS `paper.md` + `paper.bib`
  pattern), cite with `@key`, and end the document with `# References` + `::: {#refs}`.
- Copy `apa.csl` into the same directory so the render is reproducible; fetch from
  `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/citation-style-language/styles/master/apa.csl`.
- Verify author metadata against the PDF itself, not against what an agent reported.
- Never let a line start with `@key.` — pandoc parses it as an example list and renders
  a stray number instead of the citation.
- STIXGeneral has no subscript glyphs: write CO2, CH4, N2O, not CO₂, CH₄, N₂O.
